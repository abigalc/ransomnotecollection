## Data Management Plan

DSCI / LIB 350M: Humanities Research Data Management 
Authors: Teagan Sloan, Mia Middendorff, Abigail Callahan

# Section 1: Project Overview

We built a digital repository of ransom notes from 1874 to 2008 using CollectionBuilder on GitHub. This collection brings together ransom notes, newspaper coverage, photographs, and related ephemera from some of the most notorious kidnapping and ransom cases in American history that ended in murder, including the Lindbergh baby kidnapping (1932), the abduction and murder of Charles Mattson (1936), the Adolph Coors III case (1960), and others. At the heart of the collection are the ransom notes themselves, handwritten and typed demands that served as both the criminal's primary means of communication and, ultimately, key pieces of forensic evidence used to identify and convict perpetrators. Surrounding these documents are newspaper clippings that reveal how the press shaped public perception of these crimes, as well as photographs of the victims, suspects, crime scenes, and investigators involved.

The theme of this collection matters because ransom notes allow a unique intersection of crime, communication, and forensic science. These documents transformed how American law enforcement approached kidnapping investigations, contributed to the passage of the Federal Kidnapping Act ("Lindbergh Law") in 1932, and pioneered the use of questioned document examination as a forensic discipline. We hope this collection will be valuable to students of criminal justice and forensic linguistics, true crime researchers, journalism historians studying tabloid coverage of the early-to-mid twentieth century, and general readers fascinated by how these high-profile cases shaped American legal and cultural history. Visitors might use the collection to compare the linguistic and handwriting features across notes, trace how newspaper coverage evolved from one case to the next, or examine how famous victims and their families were portrayed in the press. They can also shed light on the events that may have ultimately led to the murders and allow historians to study and learn from these cases. Bringing all of the data together in one digital space allows for researchers and browsers alike to have direct downloadable access to our objects. This further allows people to be able to use our hard work to help support theirs, and it makes it a lot easier to access as opposed to all of the digging that we did in order to find these specific images. The other metadata helps to create a clearer image of the cases we covered and more insight into our curious audience, like our sources, when things were created, and the type of writing it is. The objects gathered came from publicly available digitized newspaper archives such as Chronicling America and Newspapers.com, FBI Records, and Washington State University Libraries Digital Collections, as well as other sources indirectly covering the specific cases.

# Section 5: Access, Reuse & Rights

We've licensed our original content, the metadata, written descriptions, and site copy, under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 (CC BY 4.0). This lets others copy, share, and build on our work, including for commercial use, as long as they credit our group. We chose this license because we want the collection to be useful to students, researchers, and the public without unnecessary barriers.

This license only covers what we created. The source objects themselves keep the rights assigned by their original institutions.

# Rights implications

Our sources have a mix of rights statuses, and users need to follow the terms of each individual item, not just our license.

The six FBI items and one of the CBS items are in the public domain and can be reused freely. The items from Wikisource, UCLA, and Wikimedia Commons are under Creative Commons licenses, so they can be reused with attribution (and sometimes share-alike requirements; users should check each record).

The San Francisco Chronicle, Washington State University, Pennsylvania Center for the Book, and Guardian items carry Rights Statements, meaning they're still under copyright. Three CBS items and two Find A Grave items are in copyright as well, but we can not find the original owners. We're including them under a fair use argument: our use is educational, non-commercial, transformative (placing them in a scholarly collection about ransom notes), and limited to single representative items. Anyone reusing these specific items should evaluate fair use for their own purposes.

# Ethical Considerations

This collection deals with real victims of violent crime, including children, so we want to be honest about the ethical weight that carries.
Several victims were minors, and their photographs are part of the historical record. We included them because they're essential to understanding how the press covered these cases, but users should remember these were real families whose grief was real, not just case studies.

We made deliberate choices about what to leave out. We did not include crime scene or autopsy images, even when available, because they don't add to the scholarly questions we're exploring and would feel exploitative. We also chose not to translate the German-language ransom note in the Ursula Herrmann case, since a careless translation could distort the linguistic features that make ransom notes valuable as forensic evidence.

True crime as a genre is often criticized for sensationalizing violence and centering perpetrators over victims. We've tried to avoid this by focusing the collection on the forensic, legal, and journalistic significance of ransom notes, how they shaped questioned document examination, the Federal Kidnapping Act, and crime reporting, rather than on the perpetrators or the violence itself.
